# Writing region texts

A region page has two texts under its header. Each text has one job, so a
fact belongs in one of them and never in both.

| Text | The question it answers |
|------|-------------------------|
| **Geography** | Where is this region, what is under my tyres, and what will I ride against or enjoy? |
| **History, nature and culture** | What story does this landscape tell as I ride through it? |

The geography text is about what the land is made of and how it rides. The
history, nature and culture text is about why the land looks the way it does
and what lives there.

## Where you write them

Anyone who is signed in can write, edit or translate both texts. A curator of
the region approves a text before it shows. A curator's own text inside their
area shows at once.

Both texts and the region's figures are on one page, the region editor. It
has three steps: **1 Figures**, **2 Geography** and **3 History, nature and
culture**. Change one step or all three, then press **Send** once. Each part
you change goes to the curator as its own proposal.

| Opens the region editor at | Address, for Drenthe |
|----------------------------|----------------------|
| 1 Figures | [cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/edit](https://cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/edit) |
| 2 Geography | [cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/text](https://cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/text) |
| 3 History, nature and culture | [cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/story](https://cyclingcommons.org/regions/drenthe/story) |

For another region, use the last part of its page address in place of
`drenthe`. The region page also opens the editor:

- Where the region has only the automatic text from Wikipedia, headed
  **About** and the region's name, a link under it reads **Write about the
  geography of this region**.
- The figures at the top end in a pencil, **Edit these figures**.
- A text that exists ends in a pencil, **Edit this text**.
- A region without a geography text shows **Write about the geography of this
  region**.
- A region without a history text shows **Write about the history, nature and
  culture of this region**.

Pick the language at the top of the editor. Every language has its own
texts, and the same rules hold in every language. The figures are the same
in every language.

## The geography text

Where is this region, what is under my tyres, and what will I ride against or
enjoy?

**Put in:**

- **Position and borders.** Where the region lies, its neighbours, its coast,
  and the big natural borders: rivers, seas, mountain ranges.
- **Ground and relief.** The soil (sea clay, peat, gravel, glacial sand,
  loess), how the land rises and falls (flat, rolling, short steep climbs), and
  the rivers, deltas and canals that run through it.
- **What covers the land.** Bogs, moving dunes, open farmland, dense pine
  forest, heath.
- **How it rides.** Wind exposure, the road surface (separate asphalt cycle
  paths, brick roads, gravel or cobbled farm tracks), and how you cross water:
  ferries, bridges, dikes.

**Leave out:**

- The area, the population and whether the riding is flat, hilly or
  mountainous. These are the region's figures. They show at the top of the
  page, and you set them in step 1, below.
- History: when land was drained, wars, treaties.
- Stories about culture. They belong in the other text.

**Form rules.** The geography text is one paragraph of plain text, without
links. The box counts the characters and shows the limit. Where a Wikipedia
article about the region exists, the form asks where your text comes from:
adapted from that article, or your own words. A curator decides on the
Wikipedia credit when they approve.

## The region's figures

Step 1 of the region editor, **Figures**. They show at the top of the region
page, in every language:

| Figure | What to fill in |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Area in km²** | A whole number. Leave it empty to show the area measured from the region's boundary. The form shows that measured area under the field. |
| **Population** and **Counted in** | A whole number, and the year it was counted. Give both or neither. Most regions start with the latest count on Wikidata; the form names it, for example "Source: Wikidata Q772". Change it when you know a newer official count. |
| **Ride character** | **Flat**, **Hilly** or **Mountainous**: what most riding in the region is like. |

You can send the figures with a text, or alone. A curator of the region
approves the figures separately from the texts.

**Template.** Fill in the brackets, then rewrite until it reads as your own:

<!-- CODE-ILLUSTRATIVE fill-in template for a region's geography text -->
```text
[Region name] lies in [part of the country or continent], bordered by [neighbouring regions or countries] and by [seas, mountain ranges or river corridors]. The land is [flat / gently rolling / stepped river terraces / mountainous], on [sand / clay / peat / limestone / loess]. [Main rivers, estuaries or lakes] divide it into [landscape divisions]. The land is covered by [open farmland / heath / forested ridges / tidal wetlands]. For cyclists, the region has [separate asphalt cycle paths / service roads / brick roads / unpaved tracks], and riders meet [strong coastal wind / long climbs / quiet country roads with few cars].
```

## The history, nature and culture text

What story does this landscape tell as I ride through it? The text has three
paragraphs, told like a documentary:

1. **People and land.** How people shaped the land or adapted to it: land
   reclamation, dwelling mounds (terpen), megalithic tombs, Roman roads,
   medieval forest clearance, peat digging.
2. **Power, trade and conflict.** How people used the land's resources and
   barriers: windmills, military water lines and fortresses, Hanseatic trade
   rivers, defensive floods.
3. **What a rider sees.** How the scenery changes on one ride: dunes giving way
   to peat bog, forest giving way to water, and the wildlife you can meet there,
   such as godwits or red deer.

**Form rules.**

- Start each paragraph on a new line.
- The box counts the characters a reader sees and shows the limit. Link
  addresses do not count.
- Cite a source where you use it. Select the words and press **Link the
  selected words**, or type the words in square brackets with the address
  right after:

<!-- CODE-ILLUSTRATIVE how a source link is typed in a history text -->
```text
The [Defence Line of Amsterdam](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/759) rings the city with forts.
```

- Link the words that name the subject, never "source", "link" or "here". The
  form refuses such a link.
- Link only `https` addresses. The form refuses a link that starts with
  `http://`. A site without a working https address stays unlinked: name it
  in the text.
- Link only the source itself: a government body, a water board, an archive,
  or a heritage or nature organisation. No blogs, shops or pages full of ads.
- Link the page about the fact where one exists, not the site's home page.
- Never type an address from memory. Open each page and copy its address. The
  form checks each link as you type it. When it cannot confirm a link, tick
  that you opened the page yourself and that it is the right page.
- There is no list of sources under the text. The links in the text are the
  sources.

## Example: Drenthe

The two texts below show how they complement each other without repeating a
fact.

**Geography**

> Drenthe sits on an elevated inland plateau in the north-east of the
> Netherlands, bordered by Friesland, Groningen and Overijssel, and by Germany
> to the east. The terrain rests on sand and boulder clay left by the ice
> sheets of the Saalian glaciation, which gives it gently rolling contours, the
> long low ridge of the Hondsrug, dry pine and heath uplands, and poorly
> drained stream valleys such as the Drentsche Aa. Large parts of the east are
> reclaimed peat: flat, open and scored by straight drainage ditches. For
> cyclists, Drenthe has a dense network of separate cycle paths: smooth asphalt
> paths without cars that cut straight through state forests and heaths, joined
> by quiet country roads and historic brick tracks. Traffic is light, and the
> tree cover shelters riders from the wind.

**History, nature and culture**

> Drenthe's dry sand plateau was settled thousands of years before the
> surrounding lowlands were drained, a safe upland amid immense swamps. More
> than 5,000 years ago, farmers of the Funnelbeaker culture used the huge
> boulders the ice had left behind to build megalithic tombs (hunebedden). The
> Hunebedcentrum in Borger tells their story.
>
> For centuries, Drenthe was ringed by raised bogs, most notably the Bourtanger
> Moor. From the nineteenth century, peat digging by hand and later by machine
> transformed the eastern frontier, turning wet bog into dried fuel for Dutch
> cities and industry. Today the water boards, among them
> [Waterschap Hunze en Aa's](https://www.hunzeenaas.nl/) and
> [Waterschap Drents Overijsselse Delta](https://www.wdodelta.nl/), balance
> drainage after heavy rain with keeping groundwater in the sand, so the
> farmland does not dry out and the last wetlands survive.
>
> Riding across Drenthe takes you through prehistoric and farming heritage.
> Routes follow the crest of the Hondsrug through the
> [UNESCO Global Geopark De Hondsrug](https://www.dehondsrug.nl/), past sandy
> pine forests and Neolithic burial chambers. Further west, quiet paths lead
> into [Nationaal Park Drentsche Aa](https://www.drentscheaa.nl/), one of the
> best-kept brook valleys of north-west Europe, where streams wind through
> hay meadows, oak pastures and old village greens (brinken).

## For curators

Before you approve a text, check that it keeps to its job. A geography text
must not tell the region's history, and a history text must not describe
where the region lies. When a passage is in the wrong text, correct it on the
correction form, or ask the writer to move it. The correction form shows the
same reminder at its top.
